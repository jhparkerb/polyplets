## 1. The heights it removes are the expensive ones

The a(40) run, by phase (`results/ns_a40/PROVENANCE.md`,
`results/ns_a40/rundir_size.log`). Bars are core-hours; the disk column
is the run-dir peak reached during that phase.

```
            core-hours                              cpu-h    wall      disk
  H <= 19  #################                          504    6.3h/80c    69 GB  KEPT
  H = 20   ################                           461    9.6h/48c   172 GB  REMOVED
  H = 21   ########################################  1165   36.4h/32c   363 GB  REMOVED
```

The two removed heights are **76% of the core-hours**
and take the disk peak from 363 GB to 69 GB. The
`reach-scaling-and-resourcing` memory calls the engine disk/spill-bound
and puts the home boxes' ceiling there — so this is the axis that was
actually binding.

## 2. Where each row's cells come from

One line per row, one character per height:

    #   swept, and still swept under Undertow
    X   swept ONLY because the classical rule needed it -- what Undertow
        deletes, and always the tallest, most expensive heights
    .   supplied by the diagonal tower

```
        H=1                     H=n
  a(37)  ##################X..................   sweep H<=18, was H<=19
  a(38)  ##################XX..................   sweep H<=18, was H<=20
  a(39)  ###################X...................   sweep H<=19, was H<=20
  a(40)  ###################XX...................   sweep H<=19, was H<=21
  a(41)  ###################XX....................   sweep H<=19, was H<=21
```

Two heights come off rows 40 and 41, one off rows 39 and 37: the classical
ceiling `(n+2)/2` and the Undertow ceiling `(n-3)/2` each move by one for
every two of `n`, so which rows gain two is a parity question.

Every `X` in that figure is a height whose cost is roughly 3x the one
below it. The two `X`s on row 40 are the 461 and 1165 core-hour bars of
section 1.

## 4. The rule, in one line

```
  classical:   sweep every height up to (n+2)/2,  tower above
  Undertow:    sweep every height up to (n-3)/2,  tower above

               because a level's two anchors may sit BELOW its onset,
               where the cells are shorter, instead of above it, where
               they are the tallest cells on the diagonal.
```

A height is worth about 3x in compute, so two heights is about 9x — and,
as §1 shows, it is the disk that moves furthest.
