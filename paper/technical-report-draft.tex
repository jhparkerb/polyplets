\begin{abstract}
The king-lattice polyplet sequence \oeis{A006770} has been expanded through
\[ a(40) = \num{56749893611764175164545926946127}. \]

A triangular sequence $T(n,H)$ is introduced that counts the fixed king
polyplets of size $n$ and height exactly $H \le n$, so that $a(n) = \sum_H
T(n,H)$. Above the line $H > n/2$ the triangle obeys a proved closed form,
$T(n,H) = P_{n-H}(n)\,3^{\,3H-2n-1}$ with $P_k$ an integer-valued polynomial
of degree $k$, and each $P_k$ is fixed by two computed entries. The closed form,
its range of validity and the two-cell determination are proved on paper and
formalized in Lean~4 against standard axioms.

$a(1)$--$a(18)$ were already known. $a(19)$ was computed by Redelmeier
enumeration; $a(20)$--$a(23)$ by transfer matrix; $a(24)$--$a(40)$ by
transfer matrix for the short heights and by evaluation of the closed forms
for the tall ones, whose enumeration is the most expensive. A forty-first
term is given with its evidence stated separately.

Every new $a(n)$ has been checked by a second computation that shares no code
with the first: for $n \le 22$ by Redelmeier enumeration, and for larger $n$
by a transfer matrix that colors cells instead of tracking connectivity,
with the closed forms supplying the entries above its reach.

Six OEIS entries are extended: \oeis{A006770} to $n = 40$, the one-sided
polyplets \oeis{A030233} to $n = 34$, and the free polyplets \oeis{A030222},
the bilateral \oeis{A030234}, the asymmetric \oeis{A030235} and the free
polyplets that are not polyominoes \oeis{A194596} to $n = 32$. The triangle
$T(n,H)$ is proposed as a new entry.

This work was completed with heavy assistance from Claude Code, especially
the Fable model, which wrote this document.
\end{abstract}

\section{Definitions}
\label{sec:definitions}

An \textbf{animal} is a finite, connected set of cells on a lattice; the
\textbf{size} of an animal is the number of cells that comprise it; the
\textbf{height} of an animal is the height of its bounding box. The
\textbf{king polyplets} (or polyplets) are the animals of the king lattice,
where a cell is connected to its eight neighbors in the compass directions;
the rook-connected animals, connected only through the four edge-sharing
neighbors, are the \textbf{polyominoes}. Every polyomino is a polyplet.

The polyplets can be counted with respect to the symmetries of the king
lattice:
\begin{description}
\item[Fixed] polyplets are distinct up to translation (\oeis{A006770});
\item[One-sided] polyplets are distinct up to translation and rotation
  (\oeis{A030233});
\item[Free] polyplets are distinct up to translation, rotation and reflection
  (\oeis{A030222}).
\end{description}

Within the free polyplets, those that are \textbf{bilateral} have at least one
reflection symmetry (\oeis{A030234}); those that are \textbf{asymmetric} have
no reflective symmetry (\oeis{A030235}).

$\mathbf{T(n,H)}$, $1 \le H \le n$, is the number of fixed polyplets of size
$n$ whose bounding box is of height exactly $H$; the row sums produce $a(n)$,
the number of fixed polyplets of size $n$ (\oeis{A006770}).

\paragraph{Fixed polyplets by height} Table~\ref{tab:tnh} shows $T(n,H)$ for
small $n$. Regions of this table have formulas. $T(n,n)$ counts the polyplets
with one cell per row, and since consecutive cells must be king-adjacent
there are three choices for each row after the first: $T(n,n) = 3^{\,n-1}$.
One row of two cells gives
\[ T(n,n-1) = (25n - 45)\,3^{\,n-4}, \qquad n \ge 3, \]
derived in Section~\ref{sec:formulas}. In general, when $H > n/2$
the cell placements are constrained enough to force a closed form. The general
statement is a theorem, proved on paper and checked by machine in Lean~4:

\begin{theorem}[diagonal law]
\label{thm:diag}
For every $k \ge 0$ there is a polynomial $P_k$ of degree $k$, taking integer
values at integers, with leading coefficient $25^k/k!$, such that
\[ T(n,H) = P_{n-H}(n)\,3^{\,3H-2n-1} \qquad \text{whenever } H > n/2, \]
and the identity fails at $H = n/2$ for every even $n \ge 2$. Moreover
$P_k(n) = Q_k(n) + a_k + b_k n$ where $Q_k$ is determined by $P_1, \dots,
P_{k-1}$, so two entries of the $k$-th diagonal with $H > n/2$ fix $P_k$
exactly.
\end{theorem}

The exponent $3H - 2n - 1$ is negative for $n/2 < H < (2n+1)/3$, and there
the statement is that $3^{\,2n+1-3H}$ divides $P_{n-H}(n)$ and the quotient is
$T(n,H)$; for example $T(7,4) = P_3(7)/27$.

The Lean~4 formalization covers the shape, the range $H > n/2$, the
integer values and the two-cell determination, with each $P_k$ for $k \le 18$
derived from two computed entries inside the proof assistant; it rests on
Lean's standard axioms and its compiled integer arithmetic, and nothing else
is assumed. The paper proof is sketched in
Section~\ref{sec:formulas}. The program that computes the triangle carries
$P_k$ for $k \le 19$. Each was fixed from the
two entries of smallest $n$ on its diagonal with $H > n/2$ and then checked
against every later enumerated entry on that diagonal, $171$ entries in all,
every one exact. The $k$-th diagonal is fixed once the $3k$-th row of $T(n,H)$ has been
computed: with the leading coefficient known, $P_k$ has $k$ free coefficients,
and the rows $2k+1, \dots, 3k$ supply $k$ entries with $H > n/2$.

\paragraph{Growth rate} The growth constant $\lambda = \lim_{n\to\infty}
a(n)^{1/n}$ exists and equals $\sup_n a(n)^{1/n}$ \cite{klarner1967}, so
$a(n) \le \lambda^n$ for every $n$. Given a polyplet $A$ of size $m$ and a
polyplet $B$ of size $n$, place $B$ so that its lowest, leftmost cell is
diagonally above and to the right of the highest, rightmost cell of $A$. The
two cells are king-adjacent, so the union is a polyplet of size $m+n$; every
cell of $B$ lies above every cell of $A$, so $A$ is recovered from the union
as its lowest $m$ cells. Distinct pairs therefore give distinct unions, and
$a(m+n) \ge a(m)\,a(n)$. In particular $a(40k) \ge a(40)^k$ for every $k$,
and therefore, from Table~\ref{tab:an} alone,
\[ \lambda \ge a(40)^{1/40} = 6.2208. \]
The ratios $a(n+1)/a(n)$ converge to the same $\lambda$ \cite{madras1999};
the last is $a(40)/a(39) = 6.9352$.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Redelmeier enumeration} Redelmeier's algorithm
\cite{redelmeier1981} grows animals one cell at a time from a fixed starting
cell, in an order that produces each fixed animal exactly once. The implementation records
each animal's bounding box, so it produces the triangle $T(n,H)$ rather than
just $a(n)$. Its cost is
proportional to the number of animals, so it was used to compute $a(19)$, in
two independent campaigns on two machines, and to confirm the transfer
matrix's counts up to $n = 22$; the confirmation of the row $n = 22$ took
about $120$ hours on each of three machines, split into $24\,000$ disjoint
subtrees. Beyond $n = 22$ its cost is prohibitive.

\paragraph{Transfer matrix method} Each height $H$ is an independent job. A
strip of height $H$ is processed column by column. A partial polyplet is
summarized by a signature recording, for each row of the most recent column,
whether the cell is occupied and if so which connected component of the
partial animal it belongs to, together with two flags for whether the top
and bottom rows of the strip have been reached. Two partial animals with the
same signature have the same completions, so counts are carried on
signatures. Because king adjacency lets components cross, the non-crossing
signature encodings used for polyominoes \cite{jensen2001} do not apply.

Each signature carries a vector of counts indexed by the number of cells
placed so far. Combining two records adds their vectors, so the result does
not depend on how the work was split across cores or machines. A signature is
dropped when the cells placed, plus a lower bound on the cells still needed
to reach both ends of the strip and to join the components, exceed $n$. A
signature whose next column is empty, whose cells form one component and
whose flags are both set is one fixed polyplet of height exactly $H$.

Two versions of the transition were used and checked against each other on
every cell of the rows $n \le 29$. The first transfers a whole column per
step, resolving connectivity by union-find over the new column's cells and
the old column's components. The second advances one cell per step, as the
polyomino transfer matrices do, keeping the one already-overwritten
north-west neighbor a new cell may still attach to as a carried byte in the
signature. It is between $11\times$ and $55\times$ faster at the heights
measured and computed $a(30)$--$a(41)$. The counts are exact integers, not
remainders modulo a prime: machine words of $64$ bits suffice through $a(25)$, and
$a(26)$ onward used $128$-bit words, with a check on every addition so that
an overflowed count stops the run instead of passing silently.

Several heights run at once on one machine, so that the sorting one height
does between columns overlaps another height's counting. $a(40)$ ran in three
stages on an $80$-core machine, because its three tallest heights did not fit
in memory together, for $5.3$ million CPU-seconds and a peak of $363$~GB of
disk.

\paragraph{Evaluating the tall heights} From $a(24)$ on, the heights with
$H > n/2$ whose $P_k$ had already been fixed were evaluated from $P_k$ rather
than enumerated, and that set grew as more $P_k$ were fixed: $k \le 7$ at $a(24)$
and $k \le 18$ at $a(40)$, where heights up to $21$ were still enumerated.
The enumeration of height $21$ for $a(40)$ alone took $36$ hours on $32$
cores. The tall heights are the expensive ones because a height's cost
follows its number of signatures, not its count.

\paragraph{The coloring transfer matrix} The second program computes a
different quantity: $C_H(n)$, the number of connected $n$-cell subsets of a
strip of height $H$ whose leftmost column is fixed. It never decides
connectivity. For each $n$ it computes the sum over all $n$-cell subsets $S$
of the strip of $q^{c(S)}$, $c(S)$ the number of king-connected components of
$S$, working in polynomials in $q$ truncated after the linear term. Cells are
processed one at a time and the only state kept is which recent cells share a
color: an occupied cell with no earlier occupied neighbor either adopts a
color already in use or takes a fresh one at weight $q - b$, and a cell whose
earlier neighbors carry two different colors contributes zero. The
coefficient of $q$ is $C_H(n)$. The same sum appears in statistical
mechanics as the random-cluster model \cite{fortuinKasteleyn1972}; the proof
that the program computes it is \repo{docs/proofs/cutcount-identity.md}.

$C_H$ and $T$ carry the same information. A polyplet of height $h$ fits in a
strip of height $H$ in $H - h + 1$ vertical positions, so $C_H(n) = \sum_{h
\le H} (H-h+1)\,T(n,h)$, and inverting, $T(n,H) = C_H(n) - 2C_{H-1}(n) +
C_{H-2}(n)$. Neither identity counts anything by itself: the work is in
computing $C_H$, whose states multiply with $H$ as the main program's do. The
identities only convert one program's output into the other's, which is what
makes the comparison possible. The program works modulo one prime at a time and
reassembles the exact counts by the Chinese remainder theorem: nine primes
near $2^{16}$ per height, eight of which reconstruct any value below
$2^{128}$, above every count in the table, while the ninth is left out of the
reassembly and its remainders predicted from the others as a check. It shares no code with either program above.

\paragraph{Symmetry counts}
\label{sec:symmetry}
Let $\mathrm{Fix}_g(n)$ be the number of fixed polyplets of size $n$ left
unchanged by the symmetry $g$ of the square, so that $\mathrm{Fix}_e(n) =
a(n)$ for the identity $e$. Write $r_{90}$ and $r_{180}$ for the rotations,
$h$ for a reflection in a horizontal or vertical axis and $d$ for a
reflection in a diagonal; the two reflections of each kind have the same
fixed-point count, as do the two quarter turns. Burnside's lemma
\cite{stanley2012} gives
\begin{align*}
  \text{one-sided}(n) &= \tfrac14\bigl(a(n) + \mathrm{Fix}_{r_{180}}(n)
      + 2\,\mathrm{Fix}_{r_{90}}(n)\bigr), \\
  \text{free}(n) &= \tfrac18\bigl(a(n) + \mathrm{Fix}_{r_{180}}(n)
      + 2\,\mathrm{Fix}_{r_{90}}(n) + 2\,\mathrm{Fix}_{h}(n)
      + 2\,\mathrm{Fix}_{d}(n)\bigr).
\end{align*}
The bilateral count is the number of free polyplets with at least one
reflection symmetry; it comes from the same fixed-point counts by inclusion
and exclusion over the reflections.

The fixed-point counts were computed by two programs, a direct counter of
symmetric animals and a transfer matrix restricted to them, and the two agree
wherever both were run.

Symmetric polyplets are rare, which is why the one-sided count approaches
$a(n)/4$ and the free count $a(n)/8$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}). A polyplet
left unchanged by a nontrivial $g$ is determined by its cells on one side of
the axis or center of $g$ together with the position of that axis or center.
Those cells form at most two connected pieces, of sizes $m_1$ and $m_2$ with
$m_1 + m_2 \le n/2 + 1$, so $\mathrm{Fix}_g(n)$ is at most a polynomial in
$n$ times $a(m_1)\,a(m_2)$. On the other side, applying $a(m+n) \ge
a(m)\,a(n)$ twice gives $a(n) \ge a(m_1)\,a(m_2)\,a(n - m_1 - m_2)$. So
$\mathrm{Fix}_g(n)/a(n)$ is at most a polynomial in $n$ divided by
$a(n - m_1 - m_2)$, and $n - m_1 - m_2 \ge n/2 - 1$, where $a$ grows at least
like $6.2208^{\,n-39}$ from $a(40k) \ge a(40)^k$; the ratio therefore decays
exponentially.

\paragraph{Burnside congruences} Grouping the fixed polyplets by their exact
symmetry group, the orbits of size $8$ and $4$ vanish modulo $4$, which leaves
\[ a(n) \equiv I(C_4) + I(D_2^{\mathrm{ax}}) + I(D_2^{\mathrm{diag}})
   - 2\,I(D_4) \pmod 4, \]
where $I(K)$ counts the fixed polyplets invariant under every element of the
subgroup $K$, $C_4$ is the rotations, $D_2^{\mathrm{ax}}$ the identity, the two
axis reflections and the half turn, and $D_2^{\mathrm{diag}}$ the same with
the diagonal reflections. Since $D_2^{\mathrm{ax}}$ is the subgroup that
preserves height, the same argument on one height gives $T(n,H) \equiv
I_H(D_2^{\mathrm{ax}}) \pmod 2$. The invariant counts come from an unrelated
algorithm in minutes, and both congruences hold on every $a(n)$ and every cell
for $n \le 40$. They are weak checks, two bits per row and one per cell, and
are reported as such.

\paragraph{Formulas}
\label{sec:formulas}
A polyplet of height $H$ occupies every row of its bounding box, and since
king adjacency changes the row by at most one, a row holding a single cell is
a cut: every path from below it to above it passes through that cell.

$T(n,n)$: one cell per row, consecutive cells adjacent, so after fixing the
first cell's column each next cell is left, center or right of the one
before it, $3^{\,n-1}$ in all.

$T(n,n-1)$: there are $n-1$ rows and $n-2$ junctions between rows where a
choice is made for the placement of the next cell or cells. A single cell has
exactly three options. To place $n$ cells in $n-1$ rows, one row must have
two cells, both connected to the rest: a joiner that is either a left--right
domino or a split, two cells separated by exactly one empty cell, since a
larger gap could not be bridged by the single cell of a neighboring row. The
joiner may sit at any of the $n-3$ interior rows.

For a domino joiner one
junction has $4$ possibilities rather than $3$ and the other also $4$, since
the domino may connect diagonally or orthogonally to the left or right of the
previous cell and the next cell may be placed likewise: $16(n-3)\,3^{\,n-4}$.

For a split joiner, when the split sits exactly left and right of the
previous cell there are five placements for the next cell, and otherwise
there are four placements for the split that each force one placement of the
next cell: $(4 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 5)(n-3)\,3^{\,n-4}$. Together, $25(n-3)\,
3^{\,n-4}$ for an interior joiner.

A joiner at the top or bottom has five placements, giving $2 \cdot 5 \cdot
3^{\,n-3} = 30 \cdot 3^{\,n-4}$, and
\[ T(n,n-1) = 25(n-3)\,3^{\,n-4} + 30 \cdot 3^{\,n-4} = (25n - 45)\,3^{\,n-4}. \]
Each of the four weights $16$, $9$, $4$, $1$ was confirmed by enumerating
every fixed polyplet of size $n \le 8$ and classifying its doubled row, and
the formula holds on all $38$ computed entries $3 \le n \le 40$.

The general law (Theorem~\ref{thm:diag}) is the same bookkeeping carried to
all orders. Read a polyplet row by row: rows with one cell are walk rows, and
maximal runs of rows with two or more cells are clusters. A walk row is a cut,
so a polyplet is an alternating chain of clusters and walk rows, each cluster
weighted independently of the rest. A cluster with $k$ cells beyond one per
row has at most $k$ rows, so at surplus $k$ only finitely many cluster shapes
occur, and summing the chain gives $T(n,n-k)$ as a polynomial in $n$ of degree
at most $k$ times $3^{\,n-3k-1}$ once $n \ge 2k+1$. A further resummation
shows each $k$ adds exactly two new constants, which is why two entries fix
$P_k$, and the leading coefficient $25^k/k!$ follows from $P_1 = 25n - 45$.
The full proofs are \repo{docs/proofs/diagonal-law.md} and
\repo{docs/proofs/grand-form.md}; the theorem is formalized in Lean~4.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproduce}

Re-running the enumerations behind the later terms costs CPU-months, so this
section says what stands behind each number; the checks that run in minutes
are listed at the end. A value computed twice by programs sharing no code is
confirmed. A value computed once and found consistent with proved structure
is checked, which catches gross errors and not a subtle systematic one.

\paragraph{Anchors} $a(1)$--$a(18)$ equal the eighteen terms the OEIS serves
for A006770, from both enumeration programs, and the difference between the
free polyplets and the free polyplets that are not polyominoes equals the
free polyominoes A000105 at every $n$ from $18$ to $32$.

\paragraph{Every run re-derives everything below it} Each production run
computes the whole triangle for its size from scratch rather than extending a
stored one, and every earlier term is checked against the stored value. The
run for $a(41)$ reproduces the stored triangle at every one of the $610$
entries of heights $1$--$20$ in rows $1$--$40$.

\paragraph{Second computations} Every entry of every row $n \le 22$ agrees
between Redelmeier enumeration and the transfer matrix. The coloring transfer
matrix, run to height $19$ at the size row $41$ needs, agrees with the main
program on all $589$ entries with $n \le 40$ and $H \le 19$ and on the $19$
entries of row $41$ it reaches; in every reconstruction one prime was left
out and its remainders were predicted correctly from the others. A
third program, a strip transfer matrix with the same connectivity idea as the
main program but none of its code, agrees on all $469$ entries with $H \le 14$.

\paragraph{The closed forms} Each $P_k$ was fixed from two entries and then met
later enumerated entries it had not used: $P_{15}$ at $T(33,18)$, $P_{16}$ at
$T(35,19)$, $P_{17}$ at $T(37,20)$, $P_{18}$ at $T(39,21)$. Refitting every
$P_k$ from its two entries of smallest $n$ with $H > n/2$ reproduces all $171$
other enumerated entries with $H > n/2$.

Just below that range the formula still holds up to a correction:
$T(2k+1-j,\,k+1-j) = P_k(2k+1-j)\,3^{-(k+j)} + D_j(k)$, where $D_j(k)$ is
computed from the clusters of bounded surplus without reading the triangle
(\repo{results/undertow.md}). Fixing every $P_k$, $k \le 19$, from entries of
height at most $19$, all of which the coloring program covers, reproduces
every entry of every row $n \le 39$ and sums each row to the published term
with no input from the main program. That is the sense in which
$a(23)$--$a(39)$ are confirmed.
