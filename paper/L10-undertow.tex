\begin{abstract}
\noindent
On the king lattice, the number $T(n,H)$ of fixed animals with $n$ cells and
bounding-box height exactly $H$ obeys a diagonal law: for fixed $k$ and
$n \ge 2k+1$,
\[
  T(n,\,n-k) \;=\; P_k(n)\cdot 3^{\,n-1-3k},
  \qquad \deg P_k = k,
\]
and the onset $n \ge 2k+1$ is sharp. The standard way to determine $P_k$ is to
enumerate the two tallest cells on its diagonal --- both above the onset --- and
solve. Those two cells are the most expensive computations in the whole
problem.

This paper does it from the other side. Below the onset the law is false, but
its error is exactly computable: at depth $j = 2k+1-n$ the defect $\Dj(k)$ is
produced by a bounded-excess family enumeration that never reads the triangle.
Adding it back turns a \emph{short} cell into an exact linear equation for the
tall level, and since a level carries exactly two constants --- a proved,
formalized fact --- two such equations pin it.

The consequence is a reach rule: exact depths up to $J$ give every level
attainable from $n \le 2H_{\max} + J - 1$, so each two units of depth buy a
height, and a height costs a factor of about $2.9$. In practice it moved the
two tallest cells of row $40$ inside already-banked data, and produced
\[
  a(41) \;=\; 393811462683918679824582849262105
\]
for $4.72$ hours on 40 cores, against the $36.4$ hours and $363$\,GB that
row~$40$'s single tallest cell had cost on its own.

The construction is validated outside the project on the square lattice, where
the counts are published: there the below-onset fit equals the classical one at
every level tested and reproduces the tall cell it was denied. We state what is
still soft, including one level whose pin has no holdout, and we have run no
priority pass.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Let $T(n,H)$ count fixed king-lattice animals with $n$ cells whose bounding box
has height exactly $H$. Reading the triangle along its diagonals
$k = n - H$ rather than along its rows, the counts satisfy a law that is a
theorem (\repo{docs/proofs/diagonal-law.md}, and companion paper L1 in the
general row-local form): for each fixed $k$ there is a polynomial $P_k$ of
degree $k$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:law}
  T(n,\,n-k) \;=\; P_k(n)\cdot 3^{\,n-1-3k}
  \qquad\text{for } n \ge 2k+1,
\end{equation}
and the onset is sharp: at $n = 2k$ the identity fails.

A tower built on \eqref{eq:law} is how the tall part of a row is obtained
without sweeping it. Rows are swept up to some ceiling height $H_{\max}$;
above that, each diagonal supplies its cells from $P_k$. The question is where
the $P_k$ come from.

The classical answer is to read them off the two tallest cells of the diagonal,
$T(2k+1,k+1)$ and $T(2k+2,k+2)$, which are the first two above the onset. That
is correct and it is expensive in exactly the wrong place, because those cells
are the ones a sweep reaches last: on this problem the cost of a height is a
factor of about $2.9$ and the tallest heights of a row are the two that dominate
its whole budget. Row $40$'s $H = 21$ cell alone cost $36.4$ hours on 32 cores
with a $363$\,GB disk peak.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{What this paper does.} It supplies the same $P_k$ from cells
\emph{below} the onset, where \eqref{eq:law} is false, by adding back an error
term that is known exactly. Those cells are shorter --- $j$ rows shorter at
depth $j$ --- so they sit inside data that has already been computed.

Three things have to hold, and the paper is organised around them. A level must
carry few enough constants that a couple of equations determine it
(\S\ref{sec:frame}); the error term must be computable without using the answer
(\S\ref{sec:circular}); and the whole thing must be checkable against something
outside itself (\S\ref{sec:square}).

\section{The frame: two constants, and one equation per short cell}
\label{sec:frame}

\paragraph{Two constants.} The grand form
(\repo{docs/proofs/grand-form.md}) states that the diagonal generating function
factorises as $F(n,u) = C(u)\cdot H(u)^n$, from which level $k$ carries exactly
two new constants over the levels below it. This is proved, and it is formalized
in Lean against the standard axioms --- which matters here more than it usually
would, because the entire construction is a consequence of that count being two.
Any two independent linear equations in those constants therefore determine
$P_k$ completely.

\paragraph{One equation per short cell.} Define the defect at depth
$j = 2k+1-n$ by the difference between the truth and the law:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pin}
  T(2k+1-j,\; k+1-j) \;=\; P_k(2k+1-j)\cdot 3^{\,2k-3k-j} \;+\; \Dj(k).
\end{equation}
As an identity this is a definition rearranged; it introduces no approximation
and no asymptotics. It becomes \emph{useful} exactly when $\Dj(k)$ is known by
some route that does not involve $P_k$, and then it is one linear equation in
the two unknown constants of level $k$, purchased at a cell that is $j$ rows
shorter than the classical anchors.

Two such equations, at two depths or two levels, pin the level. That is the
whole mechanism.

\section{Why it is not circular}\label{sec:circular}

This is the first question a reader should ask, and the answer is structural
rather than rhetorical: the defect is computed by a machine that cannot see the
answer.

$\Dj(k)$ comes from bounded-excess cluster-weight families --- an enumeration
over families with a bounded excess parameter, carried out by a dynamic program
that reads neither the triangle $T$ nor any $P_k$. Companion paper L8 develops
that mathematics and closes depths $1$ through $4$; this paper consumes its
output.

The separation is enforced rather than asserted. The W3 gate keeps the two
sides apart and fails if they are ever joined, and the pin script
carries three RED controls, each of which fires: a perturbed $\Dj$ breaks the
pin; the same equation offered twice is refused as singular; a corrupted lower
level breaks the pin.

\paragraph{A sharper point, from the adversarial review.} The review of this
construction (\repo{results/undertow-review-A.md} and its siblings) traced every
input to its source, and its most useful finding is one this paper should carry
in the open rather than answer defensively:

\begin{quote}
Two tower routes agreeing is \emph{not} two confirmations. Every tower statement
about $T(40,19)$ contains $D_3(21)$, and the two routes share the grand form and
most of the defect values. Only agreement against an enumeration crosses
assumption families.
\end{quote}

The audit reported in \S\ref{sec:bought} is therefore a consistency check on the
banked triangle, not an independent confirmation of it. What crosses assumption
families is the second source (a different counting rule, in every cell of the
row) and the external validation of \S\ref{sec:square}.

\section{What it buys}\label{sec:bought}

\paragraph{Reach.} With exact defects to depth $J$, the tower covers every level
reachable from
\[
  n \;\le\; 2\,H_{\max} + J - 1 .
\]
Each two units of depth buy one height, and a height is a factor of about $2.9$
in compute.

\paragraph{Row 40, with no new compute.} $P_{19}$ pins from $T(37,18)$ and
$T(36,17)$; $P_{20}$ from $T(38,18)$ and $T(37,17)$. All four cells lie inside
rows that had already been banked at $H \le 18$, so $T(40,20)$ and $T(40,21)$
became predictions from existing data rather than computations.

\paragraph{$a(41)$.} Computed 2026-08-20 in two halves
(\repo{results/a41/PROVENANCE.md}): heights $1$--$19$ by a real sweep --- wall
$16{,}998.7$\,s ($4.72$ hours) on 40 cores, peak RSS $557$\,MB --- and heights
$20$--$41$ by the tower, with $k \le 19$ from the wired table and $k = 20, 21$
pinned from below-onset cells. The result is
\[
  a(41) \;=\; 393811462683918679824582849262105 .
\]
The comparison worth making is not against the whole of row $40$ but against its
single tallest cell: $36.4$ hours on 32 cores and a $363$\,GB disk peak, for one
cell. Row $41$ did not pay it.

\paragraph{The audit.} Depths $1$--$4$ together with the two onset anchors give
six equations for two unknowns at each level, so four independent consistency
checks per level, computed from short cells through mathematics that shares
nothing with the sweep. All $18$ wired levels were re-derived exactly over every
available depth pair: $18$ of $18$. As \S\ref{sec:circular} says, this checks
the banked values against each other and the frame; it is not a second
enumeration.

\section{The external validation}\label{sec:square}

Everything above is internal to one lattice and one project. The square lattice
is where that stops being true, because its counts are published by other
people.

There the drift set has $b = |D| = 1$, so the law degenerates to a plain
polynomial and the depth-$1$ defect is
\[
  D_1(k) \;=\; (-1)^{k+1}.
\]
At every level $k = 1, \dots, 6$ the below-onset fit \emph{equals} the classical
one as a polynomial, and it \emph{reproduces the tallest cell it was denied},
exactly. The square triangle's rows sum to \oeis{A001168} before any of this is
attempted, which is what makes the input external rather than ours; a RED control
confirms that the wrong defect sign breaks the fit.

\paragraph{Its limits, stated plainly.} The first version of this check reached
$J = 1$, so the saving it demonstrates is one height rather than the king
lattice's two or three. A later measurement
(\repo{results/undertow-square-depth2.md}) established depth $2$ ab initio on
the square lattice, with held-out cells, so the identity is now checked
externally at two depths --- but no closed form for square $D_2$ emerged in five
levels, and the family DP that produces the king defects remains king-only:
adjacency is baked into its row transfer, so making it lattice-parametric is a
build and not a substitution.

\section{What is still soft}\label{sec:soft}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$P_{21}$ has one pin pair and no holdout.} Level $20$ is pinned
six ways with five independent checks. Level $21$ pins from $T(40,19)$ and
$T(39,18)$ and has nothing checking it until either depth $5$ is available or
$T(41,20)$ is swept. This is the last soft spot in the construction and
\repo{results/confidence.md} says so in the same terms.

\item \textbf{Solving a whole column is a reformulation, not extra redundancy.}
An earlier reading had it the other way. Each below-onset cell brings one
equation \emph{and} one unknown $\Dj(k)$, so the honest accounting is
\[
  \text{surplus} \;=\; (\text{banked cells whose depth has a known } \Dj) - 2,
\]
and cells at depths whose defect is unknown contribute nothing. This is the kind
of correction a referee finds, and it is better made here.

\item \textbf{Depth $5$ exists as a price, not as a table.} Its cost was
asserted at $\sim16$ hours and $\sim103$\,GB and is now measured at $3.1$ hours
and $8.5$\,GB, or $6.7$ hours and $16.1$\,GB on the pessimistic bound
(\repo{results/depth5-cost-settled.md}) --- an order of magnitude below the
assertion, which is worth recording as a caution about asserted prices in
general. The gate that must pass before $D_5$ is used at $k = 21$ is written and
is deliberately RED until the table exists.
\end{itemize}

\section{Novelty}\label{sec:novelty}

\textbf{No priority pass has been run for this paper.} Every other paper in this
series states the date of its pass and what it found; this one cannot, and the
ledger above says so rather than leaving the omission to be inferred.

That pass is a gate before circulation rather than a formality, and the reason
is specific. The mechanism here --- correcting an asymptotic law by an exactly
computable error term in order to use data from the region where the law fails
--- is a natural idea, and the literature on series analysis and on
finite-size corrections is exactly where a version of it would already live,
possibly under a name this project would not think to search for. What would be
new, if anything is, is not the idea but the combination: an error term that is
\emph{exact and independently computable}, against a law whose constant count
per level is \emph{proved}, in a setting where the saving is measured in banked
compute.

\section{Open problems}\label{sec:open}

\begin{openproblem}
Make the family DP lattice-parametric. The above-onset half of the ledger
already is: the cumulant assembly carries over to any row-local lattice by the
single substitution $3 \mapsto b = |D|$. The below-onset half does not, because
adjacency is baked into the family DP's row transfer. Doing so would give
$\Dj$ on the square lattice at every depth and turn the external check from a
validation into a second instance.
\end{openproblem}

\begin{openproblem}
Give $P_{21}$ a holdout without sweeping $H = 21$: either an exact $D_5$ at
$k = 21$, or an independent route to one of the two cells its pin uses.
\end{openproblem}

\begin{openproblem}
Is there a closed form for the square-lattice $D_2$? Five levels of exact values
produced none, which is the first place the defect's structure has looked
different away from the king lattice.
\end{openproblem}
