\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A \emph{polyplet} (also \emph{king-move animal} or \emph{kingwise-connected
polyomino}) is a finite set of unit cells of the square lattice that is
connected under king moves---two cells are adjacent if they share an edge
\emph{or} a corner. OEIS sequence \href{https://oeis.org/A006770}{A006770}
counts the \emph{fixed} (translation-distinct) polyplets of $n$ cells and was,
until now, known through $n=18$. We report
\[
  a(19) = \num{151609203011580},
\]
computed from scratch and confirmed by two algorithms that share no counting
logic: direct Redelmeier enumeration, run as two decorrelated campaigns on
different architectures and compilers, and a column transfer-matrix count over
boundary-connectivity signatures. The two methods agree exactly and both
reproduce all eighteen previously published terms. We also give the free and
one-sided companions, $\mathrm{Free}(19)=\num{18951156321090}$ (the next term of
A030222) and $\mathrm{OneSided}(19)=\num{37902303297525}$ (a sequence not
previously recorded). Beyond the methods and runs, we report a battery of
mutually corroborating cross-checks (including a perimeter-resolved Method-A
vs.\ Method-B agreement and a fault-injection test of the checks themselves), an
exact uniform random sampler, and---from a sample of $10{,}000$---the structure
of a \emph{typical} $19$-cell polyplet, which turns out to be a sparse, branchy,
corner-glued object almost never connected as a polyomino.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The enumeration of lattice animals---finite connected sets of cells on a regular
lattice---is a classical problem at the meeting point of combinatorics and
statistical physics~\cite{klarner,jensen2001}. On the square lattice the two
standard families are \emph{polyominoes}, connected through shared edges, and
\emph{polyplets}, connected through shared edges or corners. Counting the
\emph{fixed} (translation-distinct) animals of each size $n$ is a benchmark
hard-counting problem: the counts grow exponentially, so exact values are known
only for modest $n$ and each additional term is a small computational milestone.
The fixed-polyomino sequence (OEIS \href{https://oeis.org/A001168}{A001168}) has
been pushed furthest~\cite{redelmeier,jensen2003,barequet}; the fixed-polyplet
sequence (OEIS \href{https://oeis.org/A006770}{A006770}) had been recorded
through $n=18$.

This note reports and confirms the next term, $a(19)$. The work is an amateur
contribution carried out with substantial AI assistance
(Section~\ref{sec:ainote}); its value lies not in algorithmic novelty---the
ingredients are standard---but in an independent, cross-checked computation of a
previously unrecorded term. Because a single $15$-digit count produced by one
program is properly a conjecture rather than a result, the central design choice
was redundancy: the value is computed twice, by two algorithms that share no
counting logic, on different hardware and compilers.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item the value $a(19)=\num{151609203011580}$, with the full height
        decomposition (Tables~\ref{tab:terms},~\ref{tab:byheight});
  \item a king-adjacency column transfer matrix using a whole-column transfer
        step and an admissible size-budget prune (Section~\ref{sec:methodB}),

  \item a confirmation protocol---two algorithms agreeing exactly, both

  \item an exact uniform random sampler for $n$-cell polyplets with an
        independent verifier (Section~\ref{sec:sampling}); and
  \item open code, data, and a provenance ledger
        (Section~\ref{sec:availability}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Outline.} Section~\ref{sec:object} fixes definitions;

\section{Definitions and notation}
\label{sec:object}

Fix the square lattice $\mathbb{Z}^2$ and call its unit squares \emph{cells}. A
\emph{lattice animal} is a finite, nonempty set of cells that is connected under
some fixed adjacency. Two adjacencies dominate the literature:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rook} (edge / $4$-)adjacency: cells sharing a side. Animals
        connected this way are \emph{polyominoes}.
  \item \textbf{King} (edge-or-corner / $8$-)adjacency: cells sharing a side or
        a corner. Animals connected this way are \emph{polyplets}.
\end{itemize}

Every polyomino is a polyplet, but not conversely: two cells touching only at a
corner form a valid polyplet and not a polyomino. Polyplets are thus the strictly
larger family, and the diagonal contacts are exactly what distinguishes them
(Figure~\ref{fig:specimen}).

One subtlety bears noting, since it governs what counts as a genuinely new
shape. A polyplet connected \emph{purely} by diagonal contacts lives entirely on
one colour class of the checkerboard---a diagonal step preserves $(c+r)\bmod 2$
---and that colour class, under diagonal adjacency, is isomorphic (rotate
$45^\circ$, rescale by $\sqrt2$) to the square lattice under edge adjacency. So a
purely diagonal polyplet is a polyomino in disguise. A polyplet escapes this only
by using \emph{both} colours, which forces at least one genuine edge contact;
the honestly-king shapes are those that mix edge and corner adjacencies.

Animals may be counted under three equivalence relations, which must not be
conflated:
\begin{description}
  \item[Fixed] up to translation only; rotations and reflections are
        \emph{distinct}.
  \item[One-sided] up to translation and rotation, but not reflection.
  \item[Free] up to translation, rotation, and reflection.
\end{description}

\noindent
A006770 is the \textbf{fixed} count. The cleanest demonstration is $a(2)=4$: the
two-cell polyplets are the horizontal domino, the vertical domino, and the
\emph{two} diagonal dominoes---which are reflections of one another and are
counted separately. (The corresponding free count is
\href{https://oeis.org/A030222}{A030222}, with $\mathrm{Free}(2)=2$; the fixed
\emph{polyomino} count is \href{https://oeis.org/A001168}{A001168}.)

\paragraph{The problem.} Determine $a(19)$, the number of fixed polyplets of $19$
cells. The number of lattice animals grows exponentially
($a(n)\sim C\lambda^{n}n^{-1}$ for a lattice-dependent growth constant
$\lambda$~\cite{klarner,jensen2001}), so $a(19)$ is a $15$-digit number that
cannot be reached by listing animals individually; it must be \emph{counted}
without enumeration, then cross-checked.

\section{Result}
\label{sec:result}

\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\,a(19) = \num{151609203011580}\,}
\end{equation}

The full sequence through $n=19$ is given in Table~\ref{tab:terms}; terms
$n\le 18$ reproduce A006770 exactly, and $n=19$ is new. The growth ratio
$a(19)/a(18)=\num{151609203011580}/\num{22471158811164}\approx 6.7468$ continues
the slow monotone climb of successive ratios (e.g.\ $a(18)/a(17)\approx 6.7272$)
toward the king-lattice growth constant $\lambda$. The shortfall from $\lambda$ is
the $n^{-1}$ correction: $a(n)/a(n-1)=\lambda\,(1-1/n)(1+o(1))$, so inverting the
last two ratios gives the rough empirical estimate $\lambda\approx 7.1$.

\section{The free and one-sided companions}
\label{sec:companions}

The same cell count, counted under coarser equivalences, yields two further new
terms:
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{Free}(19)      &= \num{18951156321090}, &
  \mathrm{OneSided}(19)  &= \num{37902303297525}.
\end{align}
$\mathrm{Free}(19)$ is the next term of OEIS A030222 (free polyplets, previously
known to $n=17$); the one-sided count has \emph{no} OEIS sequence at all, so that
entire column is new.

These ride on the fixed count for free, by Burnside's lemma over the square's
symmetry group $D_4$:
\[
  \mathrm{Free}(n) = \tfrac{1}{8}\bigl(\mathrm{Fixed} + 2R_{90} + R_{180}
                     + 2H + 2D\bigr),\quad
  \mathrm{OneSided}(n) = \tfrac{1}{4}\bigl(\mathrm{Fixed} + 2R_{90} + R_{180}\bigr),
\]
where $R_{90},R_{180},H,D$ count the animals fixed by each symmetry. Those
symmetric sub-counts are computed by a dedicated enumerator: a $g$-symmetric
animal is determined by a fundamental domain, so they are $\sqrt{\,}$-rare and
reach $n=19$ in seconds. Confidence:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the assembled free counts reproduce A030222 \emph{exactly} for all
        $n\le 17$ (the entire published range);
  \item the $n=19$ symmetric sub-counts are reproduced by an \emph{independent
        Python reimplementation} matching the C++ counter exactly
        ($R_{180}=\num{10178520}$, axis $=\num{9765524}$, diagonal
        $=\num{8923786}$, and $R_{90}$ is empty since a $90^\circ$-symmetric
        animal needs size $\equiv 0,1 \pmod 4$ and $19\equiv 3$) --- a
        different-language reimplementation agreeing catches errors a recompile
        would not;
  \item sanity: $\mathrm{Free}(19)$ exceeds $a(19)/8$ by only ${\sim}6\times10^6$,
        the expected small positive symmetric correction.
\end{itemize}
Since $\mathrm{Free}/\mathrm{OneSided}$ are exact rational combinations of the
confirmed $a(19)$ and the symmetric counts, they inherit that confidence; full
hardware/ISA decorrelation of the symmetric counts is the one remaining step.

\section{Method A: direct enumeration}
\label{sec:methodA}

The first method is Redelmeier's backtracking enumeration~\cite{redelmeier},
adapted to king adjacency. Starting from a single anchored cell, the algorithm
grows every animal exactly once by maintaining an ordered frontier of
\emph{untried} neighbouring cells and recursing on each, with the standard
discipline (a cell, once placed or rejected at a given level, is excluded from
deeper levels) that guarantees each fixed animal is generated precisely once. The
recursion tallies animals by size as it goes; no animal is ever stored.

Enumeration is embarrassingly parallel by sub-tree, so the count was run as two
\emph{decorrelated} campaigns:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{a19-A} on \texttt{gympie} (Apple silicon, Apple~clang), frontier
        split $7\times 64$;
  \item \textbf{a19-B} on \texttt{ayr} (AMD Threadripper 2990WX, GCC~12.2/x86),
        frontier split $8\times 96$.
\end{itemize}
The two differ in instruction-set architecture, compiler, hardware, and
sub-problem decomposition. Their per-size output files are
\textbf{byte-identical} (SHA-256 \texttt{558f0bdf\ldots} on both). Because the
arithmetic is exact integer addition, a shared hardware, compiler, or scheduling
fault could not have produced identical \emph{wrong} digits; the only error mode
the two campaigns cannot jointly catch is a bug in the shared \emph{algorithm}.
That is what Method~B exists to rule out.

\section{Method B: the column transfer matrix}
\label{sec:methodB}

The second method counts the same animals by an entirely different principle:
dynamic programming over the connectivity of a moving boundary, with no animal
ever materialised. It is a king-adjacency adaptation of the transfer-matrix /
finite-lattice technique used for polyominoes and lattice
animals~\cite{jensen2001,jensen2003,bousquet}.

\subsection{Decomposition by height}
Every animal has a bounding box of some height $H$ (number of occupied rows),
$1\le H\le n$. Counting those of height \emph{exactly} $H$ separately gives
\begin{equation}
  a(n) \;=\; \sum_{H=1}^{n} \byH(n),
  \qquad
  \byH(n) := \#\{\text{fixed polyplets of } n \text{ cells, bounding-box height } H\}.
  \label{eq:byheight}
\end{equation}
The heights are independent sub-problems---separately computable, checkpointable,
and parallelisable. Table~\ref{tab:byheight} lists $\byH(19)$.

\subsection{Sweeping a strip column by column}
Confine the animal to a horizontal strip of $H$ rows and process columns left to
right. A \emph{boundary signature} records everything about the columns placed so
far that can affect the future:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the occupancy of the most recent column (which of rows $0,\dots,H-1$ are
        filled);
  \item a partition of those filled cells into the connected components of the
        partial animal; and
  \item two flags: whether any cell has yet appeared in row $0$ (top) and in row
        $H-1$ (bottom).
\end{enumerate}
Earlier columns are forgotten: a future cell attaches only to the current column
(one step left) or to later cells, so the signature is a Markov state and the DP
is exact.

\paragraph{Why a whole column at a time.} King adjacency includes the four
diagonals, so deciding cell $(c,r)$ requires the diagonal neighbour $(c{-}1,r{\pm}1)$.
A cell-at-a-time sweep would already have overwritten it. Transferring an
\emph{entire column} at once keeps the previous column wholly present, so all king
adjacencies (old rows $r{-}1,r,r{+}1$ to new row $r$) are available with no carry;
connectivity across the step is then resolved by union--find, which handles the
$k$-way merges and crossing partitions a single new cell can induce without
special cases. This whole-column king transfer is the one genuinely new ingredient
relative to the rook-adjacency literature.

\subsection{Counting each animal once}
Three invariants together make accepted sweeps biject with fixed polyplets:
\emph{(i)} the first placed column is column $0$ (the animal's leftmost cells sit
there, so no animal is counted at several horizontal offsets); \emph{(ii)} a
completed animal is tallied only when both touch-flags are set (height exactly
$H$, so~\eqref{eq:byheight} does not double-count across heights); \emph{(iii)} a
completed animal is tallied only when its boundary is a single component, while a
partial state in which some component can never reconnect is killed immediately.
The DP carries, per signature, a vector of counts by cell-count; closing a single
top-and-bottom-touching component harvests those counts into $\byH(\cdot)$.

\subsection{The admissible pruning}
A size-budget prune drops any partial state whose minimum cells-so-far plus an
\emph{admissible} lower bound on cells-still-required exceeds $n$. The lower bound
sums three forced, disjoint contributions: the vertical reach needed to touch an
untouched top or bottom row, and the width of any empty row-band that lies between
occupied rows and is spanned by \emph{no} single component (such a band must be
bridged by a future king path). Because the bound never over-estimates, the prune
can only ever cause a count to come out \emph{low}, never high; consequently
reproducing every published term (Section~\ref{sec:confirm}) is a complete
certificate that the prune dropped no real animal in range.

\subsection{Engineering}
The hot path uses a fixed-width signature (supporting $H\le 30$) as the key of a
flat open-addressing hash map with counts stored contiguously, a stack-allocated
union--find, and a viable-mask generator that enumerates only the column patterns
worth trying (nonzero, non-stranding, in budget) rather than all $2^H$. Heights
are checkpointed as they finish (a crash costs one height, not the run) and were
executed as independent per-height jobs. Within a single height the sweep is also
multithreaded by sharding the state map under per-shard locks; this is
bit-identical to the serial sweep but, being memory-bandwidth bound, scales only
to $\sim 2\times$, so the production run relied on cross-height parallelism
instead.

\section{Confirmation and cross-checks}
\label{sec:confirm}

The two methods agree, and several independent checks back the value:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{External truth.} Both methods reproduce A006770 \emph{exactly} for
        every $n\le 18$, the entire previously published range.
  \item \textbf{Method A $=$ Method B.} The transfer-matrix totals
        $a(n)=\sum_H \byH(n)$ are byte-identical to the Redelmeier campaign output
        (same SHA-256 \texttt{558f0bdf\ldots}), so $a(19)$ and all lower terms
        agree across the two algorithms.
  \item \textbf{Forward vs.\ backward DP.} A separate \emph{backward} completion
        DP (Section~\ref{sec:sampling}), run on a different host, compiler, and
        ISA, reproduced a representative height term,
        $B_{17}(19)=\num{47839787379}$, identically to the forward count.
  \item \textbf{Structural marginals.} The transfer matrix's
        (height,\,size) marginals match the bounding-box-height marginals of the
        enumeration engine---agreement on the finer structure, not only the
        totals.
  \item \textbf{Independent subset check.} Of the generated polyplets, those
        connected under \emph{edge} (rook) adjacency reproduce A001168 (fixed
        polyominoes) exactly through $n=14$; those connected under \emph{corner}
        (bishop) adjacency do too, and the two are equal at every $n$ (the
        $45^\circ$ colour-class invariant of Section~\ref{sec:object}). The
        perimeter$=4n$ slice (no rook edges) likewise equals A001168 --- so the
        perimeter and connectivity checks corroborate each other.
  \item \textbf{Joint (size,\,perimeter) distribution.} Carrying perimeter as a
        second index, the transfer matrix's full (size,\,perimeter) distribution
        equals the generator's through $n=12$. Unlike the subset checks, this
        exercises Method~B's machinery, not just Method~A's, at finer grain than
        the totals.
  \item \textbf{Fault injection.} A wall of passing checks is ambiguous, so we
        confirmed the suite's discriminating power by mutation testing: planted
        errors are caught (a dropped diagonal diverges at $n=2$, a doubled count
        at $n=1$, a one-off in the prune at $n=10$), while a provably
        count-preserving change (reordering the neighbour offsets) correctly
        survives. The checks fail on wrong programs and pass on right ones.
  \item \textbf{Implementation hygiene.} Both engines are
        AddressSanitizer/UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer clean; the multithreaded
        transfer-matrix path is ThreadSanitizer clean and bit-identical to the
        serial path.
\end{itemize}
Two algorithms that share no counting logic agreeing to all $15$ digits, each
reproducing eighteen prior terms, is the basis for the value.

Method~A (enumeration) is the cheaper computation and completed within hours per

\section{Uniform random polyplets}
\label{sec:sampling}

A counting DP is also an exact uniform sampler~\cite{nijenhuis,flajolet}: if one
knows, for every boundary signature and every residual cell budget, the number of
ways to \emph{complete} it into a valid $n$-cell height-$H$ animal, then walking
the transfer matrix forward---at each column choosing the next column with
probability proportional to the completions it admits---emits a uniformly random
animal in one pass, at cost one walk rather than a traversal of all $a(n)$. We
compute the completion counts $W(\text{sig},\,\text{rem})$ by a memoised
\emph{backward} recursion reusing the same transition and admissible bound; as a
by-product $W(\text{seed},n)=\byH(n)$, which is the forward-vs-backward
cross-check of Section~\ref{sec:confirm}. The height itself is drawn first, with
probability $\byH(19)/a(19)$.

Each emitted specimen is then re-checked by an \emph{independent} verifier sharing
no code with the DP: a fresh king-BFS for single-connectedness, plus
bounding-box, anchoring, and cell-count tests, plus de-duplication. Across the
validation runs every sampled animal passed, and the empirical height histogram
agrees with $\byH(19)/a(19)$ under a $\chi^2$ test (a counter-vs-sampler
consistency check). We note that sampling certifies \emph{soundness}---that the
counted objects are genuine, distinct polyplets---but not the cardinality itself,
which rests on the agreement of Sections~\ref{sec:methodA}--\ref{sec:confirm}.

\subsection{What a typical 19-cell polyplet looks like}
\label{sec:typical}

A uniform sample is the right instrument for \emph{typical} structure, and the
portrait (10{,}000 specimens) is strikingly unlike the tidy blob one pictures for
a polyomino. The average $19$-cell polyplet is a \textbf{sparse, branchy,
corner-glued} object:
\begin{itemize}
  \item it shatters into ${\sim}10$ edge-connected pieces (mean rook-components
        $10.1$, peak $9$--$11$), held together mostly by diagonals --- $59\%$ of
        all its contacts are corner contacts;
  \item it is essentially \emph{never} an honest polyomino: a rook-connected
        specimen has probability $A001168(19)/a(19)\approx 4\times10^{-5}$, so the
        sample contains none --- the rarity is real, and only exact counts see it;
  \item it is fragile, not solid: a mean of $11.3$ of its $19$ cells are cut
        points (removal disconnects it) and $0\%$ are biconnected; cell degrees
        cluster at $2$--$3$;
  \item it fills only ${\sim}25\%$ of a roughly-square bounding box (mean density
        $0.25$, aspect $1.4$), and \textbf{$28\%$ enclose at least one hole};
  \item its checkerboard colours stay balanced ($|{\#}\text{even}-{\#}\text{odd}|$
        usually $1$--$5$), confirming they are genuine two-colour polyplets, not
        single-colour polyominoes in disguise.
\end{itemize}
This is the qualitative reason $a(n)$ so vastly exceeds the polyomino count:
king-adjacency lets an animal dangle many pieces from corner contacts, so the
typical one does. (The height cap $\le 13$ biases only the height-dependent
figures slightly; rare classes such as polyominoes and symmetric animals are
below the sample's resolution and belong to exact counting.)

\section{Limitations and further work}
\label{sec:further}

\paragraph{Limitations.} The argument for $a(19)$ is computational, not a proof:
it rests on two implementations agreeing and on both reproducing the published
range, which makes an undetected error require the \emph{same} fault in two
programs that share no counting logic. We regard that as strong evidence but not
a certificate. The uniform sampler certifies only soundness of the counted
objects, not the cardinality (Section~\ref{sec:sampling}). The growth-constant
remark in Section~\ref{sec:result} is a consistency observation, not an estimate.
We claim no novelty for the underlying methods, which are standard; the
contribution is the specific verified computation.

\paragraph{Further work.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Extend the count.} $a(20)$ and beyond are within reach of the
        transfer matrix given more cross-height parallelism; the per-height cost
        is governed by slack $n-H$, which the present pruning already controls.
  \item \textbf{Confirm the companions.} $\mathrm{Free}(19)$ and
        $\mathrm{OneSided}(19)$ are reported in Section~\ref{sec:companions} and
        already reproduced by two independent implementations; the one remaining
        step to put them on the same fully-confirmed footing as $a(19)$ is an
        ISA-decorrelated run of the symmetric counters on a different
        architecture.
  \item \textbf{A better sampler.} The present sampler builds every height before
        emitting; an online, one-pass design---weighted-reservoir height
        selection~\cite{efraimidis} over per-height counts streamed from the
        count, with sampling riding directly on the forward DP---would emit
        specimens from the first second and spend effort in proportion to
        probability mass rather than, as here, mostly on near-empty tall heights.
  \item \textbf{Scaling of polyplet statistics.} Section~\ref{sec:typical}
        reports the typical structure at $n=19$; the natural extension is the
        behaviour \emph{across} $n$ --- the radius-of-gyration size exponent, the
        decay of the rook-connected fraction, and the growth of hole density ---
        which needs uniform samples at several sizes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Concluding remarks}
\label{sec:remarks}

The methodological point is the familiar one that at fifteen digits a single
program's output should be treated as a conjecture, and that confidence comes
from redundancy without shared assumptions: two algorithms whose only common
ground is the definition of the object, run on different architectures and
compilers, agreeing exactly while jointly reproducing every prior term. The
transfer matrix is valuable here not for speed---it is slower than direct
enumeration---but because its failure modes are disjoint from those of
enumeration.

The height decomposition is also informative in its own right: the distribution
of $\byH(19)$ peaks at $H=9$ and has the exact closed form $\byH(H)=3^{H-1}$ at
its extreme, and the observation that computational difficulty tracks the slack
$n-H$ rather than the height itself was what made the production schedule
practical.

\section{Data and code availability}

The source code (both enumeration and transfer-matrix engines, the sampler, and
the test gates), b-files for $n=1,\dots,19$ of A006770 and A030222, the
per-height rows $\byH(19)$, the $10{,}000$-specimen uniform sample, and a JSON

claim here against the published terms with the included harness.

This is an amateur contribution by an unaffiliated author, carried out in close
collaboration with an AI assistant (Anthropic's Claude). The AI assisted with

the drafting of this manuscript; the author set the goals and constraints, made
the design and verification decisions, owns and operated the machines on which
the computations ran, inspected the results, and is responsible for the
correctness of what is reported. The verification protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:confirm}---two independent algorithms, agreement across all
